# Reader diagnosis (the Schwartz material)

Support for **step 2 of the loop** — entering the reader's moment. Load it when
you can't tell what the reader already knows, when every candidate line sounds
like a rival's, or when you're writing more than a headline.

This is not a parallel system to run alongside the loop. It answers one
question: *which true thing goes first, for this reader, in this market.* The
loop still decides everything else, and the finishing gates still run last.

Source: Eugene M. Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966), condensed into
working terms. His examples are 1966 direct mail; the diagnosis outlives them,
the hyperbole doesn't. Treat the stages as a rough map of a market's life, not
as a measurement — the boundaries are judgement calls, and real markets sit
between rungs more often than on them.

---

## The premise everything hangs on

Copy cannot create desire. The wanting is already out there, built by forces no
ad budget can move. Your job is to take an existing mass desire and point it at
one product.

Two consequences you can act on today:

- **Fighting a trend is expensive and usually loses.** If the market wants X and
  your product is best at Y, selling X is the shorter path. Pick the desire
  before you pick the words.
- **The desire you pick decides the ceiling.** Rank candidate desires on three
  dimensions before choosing: *urgency* (how badly, right now), *staying power*
  (does it come back, or is it satisfied once), and *scope* (how many people).
  Most weak campaigns picked a real desire that was small or already sated.

And the split that most product marketing gets wrong:

- **The physical product** is what you ship — the code, the steel, the
  ingredients. It rarely closes a sale on its own.
- **The functional product** is what it does in someone's week. That's the sale.

Physical facts can't carry a headline. They have exactly four jobs: justify the
price, document the quality, promise the performance lasts, and — most
valuable — give an old claim a *new reason to be believed*.

## Ladder A — The five stages of awareness

Where the reader's head is when your line arrives. This decides what your
opening line is *about*.

Schwartz numbers these from most aware down to unaware; today's marketers
usually count the other way. Ignore the numbers and read the direction: **the
less the reader already knows, the less your opening line can lean on the
product, and the more it has to be about them.**

### 1. Most aware
Knows the product, knows what it does, wants it, hasn't bought yet.
**Headline:** name and offer. "Framer Pro — $10/mo, was $20."
**Body:** short. Best points, then the button.
**Warning:** creativity here destroys value. Don't make them decode a pun when
their card is already out.

### 2. Product aware
Knows your product exists. Isn't convinced, or doesn't know the latest.
**Headline:** product + exactly one superiority. Pick from: reinforce the
desire, sharpen how it works, extend where/when it applies, add new proof,
announce a new mechanism, announce a limit removed, or change the category it
competes in.
**Warning:** listing three superiorities gets you zero. One.

### 3. Solution aware
Wants the outcome. Doesn't know a product like yours exists.
**Headline:** name the outcome, plainly. This is the home of "How to ___."
**Body:** name the desire → prove the outcome is achievable → show your product
is where the achieving happens.
**Warning:** this stage tempts you into cleverness you haven't earned. If the
plain statement of the outcome is still fresh in this market, use it.

### 4. Problem aware
Feels the problem. Doesn't yet believe a fix exists, or doesn't connect it to
you.
**Headline:** the problem — named more precisely than they can name it
themselves. Often the problem plus the limitation you remove ("...without X"),
which is one of the most durable headline shapes there is.
**Body:** dramatize the problem until they feel the cost, then arrive as the
inevitable solution.

### 5. Unaware
No problem in mind. Or one they won't say out loud. Or a feeling with no words
yet.
**Headline:** identification. You may not mention price, product, function, or
desire — all four are meaningless to someone who isn't in the market yet. What
remains is the reader.
**Body:** a ladder of images from "that's me" → "so that's the problem" → "so
there's a fix" → "and it's this."
**Warning:** this is where most bad copy is born. A stage-5 brief is a license
to write an empty headline that's merely startling, cute, or pretty. The test:
**your reader must identify with the line before they can buy from it.** It has
to reject as many people as it attracts. If it attracts everyone, it's air.

**Modern mapping:** stages 3 and 4 are what today's marketers call
solution-aware and problem-aware; Schwartz called them desire and need. Same
ladder.

## Ladder B — The five stages of sophistication

How many rivals got there first. This decides what *kind* of thing your
headline is.

| Stage | Situation | Move |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | You're first with this claim | State it plainly. Don't decorate it. |
| 2 | Rivals repeat your claim | Intensify or enlarge it — sharper, bigger, more specific, further. A number is one way, not the only one. |
| 3 | Claims stop being believed | Lead with the mechanism — *how* it works — and let the claim follow |
| 4 | Rivals copied your mechanism | Extend the mechanism: easier, faster, fewer limits, one more thing it handles |
| 5 | Market has stopped listening | Identification — who the reader is, rather than what they get |

Notes that matter in practice:

- **Stages 2 and 4 both end the same way.** Escalation runs into the ceiling of
  belief: claims get discounted on sight, and eventually regulators take an
  interest. When enlargement stops working, you need a *different kind* of
  thing, not a bigger one.
- **Stage 3 inverts the headline.** The claim compresses into a word or two, and
  *how it works* takes the space. Restate the claim in full right below, so
  nobody loses the promise.
- **The two ladders meet at the end.** A market that never knew and a market
  that stopped listening both get identification copy — same move, different
  reason.
- **New mechanisms get harder to sell over time.** Every "new way" that came
  before you raises the bar for what reads as believable.

**Diagnosing where you are:** read enough rival pages to see the pattern — a
handful is usually enough, and if they disagree wildly that itself is the
finding. Mostly bare claims means the claim still works. Mostly escalation
means the claim is wearing out. Mostly mechanisms means yours needs an edge.
Mostly mood and a two-word slogan means the market has tuned the category out.
These are signals to weigh, not a score to compute; a market can also be
genuinely young in a niche and exhausted in the mainstream at the same time.

## Verbalization — 38 moves, grouped

Once you know *what* to say, these are ways to say it harder. They are
strengtheners, not idea-generators; reaching for them before you have the idea
is how you get slop. Grouped by what they do:

**Make it bigger or more precise**
Measure the size. Measure the speed. Compare it to something. Show how much
work it does ("all 7 nasal passages"). Metaphorize it ("melts away").

**Make it land in the body**
Make them feel, smell, hear, or see it. Demonstrate with one prime example
(the loudest noise in a Rolls-Royce is the clock). Dramatize the result as a
scene.

**Make it fresh again**
State it as a paradox ("beat the races by picking losers"). Startle them by
contradicting how they assume the mechanism works. Accuse your own claim of
being too good. Challenge a belief they hold ("you are twice as smart as you
think").

**Remove the wall**
Strip a limitation ("without surgery"). Show former limits now overcome.
Impose a limit anyone clears ("if you can count to eleven..."). Substitute for
an unpleasant task. Prevent a future problem.

**Borrow belief**
Tie in authority. Associate with people they want to be. Case-history
quotation. Before/after. State the difference. Name the mechanism in the
headline.

**Pull them in**
Ask it as a question. Offer information ("what everybody ought to know
about..."). Turn it into a challenge they must resolve. Address one specific
person ("to the man who will settle for nothing less than..."). Address the
people who *can't* buy. Warn them about a pitfall. Give the problem a name.

**Sharpen the words themselves**
Break it into two sentences. Repeat part of it. Condense by swapping in the
thing you replace ("a ring and piston job in a tube"). Symbolize with a
parallel reality ("the Atlantic becomes one-fifth as long"). Dramatize how hard
it was to make.

Invent your own. The list is not the point; the habit of trying twenty shapes
before settling is.

## The seven techniques (body copy)

Reach for these by symptom.

### 1. Intensification — *they don't want it enough yet*
Take one desire and show it fulfilled from angle after angle. The moves, in
rough order of use: describe it in full detail → put it in action → put the
reader inside the action → turn it into a test they can run → stretch the
benefits out over weeks and months → bring in an audience who reacts → bring in
experts who are surprised → contrast against the old way → paint the black side
of not having it → show how easy it is to get → use analogy where fact runs dry
→ catalog everything at the end → turn the guarantee into a final summary.

Rule: **never repeat, always reinforce.** If a new paragraph is the same angle
in new words, cut it — that's where readers leave.

### 2. Identification — *they want it but it isn't "for them"*
People buy roles, not just outcomes: character roles (adjectives they want to
be) and achievement roles (titles they've earned and want seen).

The hard constraint: **you cannot contradict the image a product already has.**
Try, and you break belief in the performance claims too. Instead: take the
primary image, dial it up if it helps, dial it down if it hurts — and use it as
a bridge to bigger images. Never ask the reader to jump a believability chasm;
build the bridge starting from their side.

### 3. Gradualization — *every claim is true and nobody believes it*
Belief is structural. A claim's power = its content × the preparation laid
before it. So: start with something they already accept, and build a chain of
small agreements toward the claim you actually need.

The toolkit: **inclusion questions** (which they answer yes to), **detailed
identification** (symptoms so specific they know you've been there),
**contradiction of a false belief** (with authority behind it), the **language
of logic** ("here's why," "therefore," "this has been proved by"), **syllogistic
structure** (A causes B causes C, so bigger A = better C), **contingency
framing** ("if X, then Y"), **paragraph parallelism** (borrow the acceptance of
the previous shape for the new claim), and **promise → proof → promise**
alternation so they never get room to argue.

The counterintuitive one: **your strongest claim often makes a weak headline.**
If it can't be believed on sight, split-test it against a smaller, more credible
opening — then earn your way back to the big claim by paragraph six.

### 4. Redefinition — *there's an objection in the way*
Three drawbacks, one tool.
- **Too hard** → simplify by renaming. Break the scary thing into a category of
  small true things ("repairs" become "adjustments"; grammar becomes "25
  mistakes, 15 minutes a day"). Not a claim of ease — a re-description that
  makes ease obvious.
- **Too small** → escalate. Show where else in their life it pays off; hook it
  to something they already treat as important.
- **Too expensive** → change the comparison. Price against what it replaces,
  what it would have cost, or what it saves — never against rivals in its own
  category.

The elegant version is a flip: turn the liability itself into the proof. Bad
medicinal smell becomes evidence the soap is strong enough.

### 5. Mechanization — *"okay, but how does it actually work?"*
The demand arrives on schedule, usually right after your promise starts sounding
too good. Answer it at the right size:
- They already know the mechanism → **name** it and move on to price.
- They don't → **describe** it, in selling language. Reason-why copy is still
  copy; it must never read as a spec sheet.
- Everyone's promise sounds alike → **feature** it. Mechanism becomes the
  headline.

Also: **a price cut needs a mechanism.** Announce a discount with no reason and
nobody believes it. Give the reason first, and the same discount lands ten times
harder.

### 6. Concentration — *they're loyal to something else*
Attacking alternatives works only when you supply the fix in the same breath.
Attack without a solution reads as spite.

Two structures: **one-for-one contrast** (bad / good, bad / good, down a list of
factors), or **time sequence** (here's what happens to you now → here's what
happens instead). Both end by handing back to intensification.

### 7. Camouflage — *the market's reflex is "this is an ad"*
Borrow trust from where it's already stored.
- **Format** — match the medium's typography, subheads, and layout so there's no
  gear-change from content to ad.
- **Phraseology** — adopt the idiom of the thing people already trust there
  (report, changelog, field note, newsletter).
- **Mood** — two escapes from the hard-sell reflex: *understatement* (few
  adjectives, short sentences that fall at the end, willingness to stop) and
  *deadly sincerity* (name a real flaw up front; the benefits after it land far
  harder).

Use honestly: camouflage means matching a register, not disguising an ad as
editorial in a way that deceives. Label ads as ads.

## The finishing layer

- **Verification** — proof is placed, not dumped. The same statistic is dull
  early and gripping the moment the reader is begging for a solution. Never a
  box labeled "Proof."
- **Reinforcement** — two claims side by side can hit ten times harder than
  either alone. Avis: "We're only No. 2" + "so we try harder." Hunt for the
  juxtaposition.
- **Interweaving** — no sentence should carry only one job. Pack promise, image,
  logic, and emotion into the same line. Then you can say twice as much in the
  same space.
- **Sensitivity** — feel the moment the reader turns and switch copy type there.
  The dead feeling mid-draft is the signal, not a block.
- **Momentum** — end thoughts incomplete on purpose. Raise a question, imply the
  answer is below, and they read on. Every momentum-builder must also sell.
- **Mood** — words and rhythm carry emotion the reader never notices. Two
  families: image-sharpeners (what they see) and emotion-definers (what to feel
  about it). The moment a reader *notices* the emotion-definers, the copy reads
  as corny and you've lost them. Read it aloud; the rhythm is half the effect.

## The three levels of creativity

Schwartz's ranking of how writers find ideas — and the reason this whole
document exists:

1. **Word substitution.** Take a proven headline, swap in your product. Works
   only if the product, market, and moment are identical to the original.
   Otherwise you've written an echo — an ad that reminds people of something
   else. **This is exactly what a language model does by default.**
2. **Formulas.** Pour the idea into a memorized mold. Useful for *phrasing*
   (that's what the 38 moves are). Useless for *conception* — the idea demands
   its own shape.
3. **Analysis.** No answers, only questions and compasses. Break the problem
   into parts, find the part that matters, start there.

The cardinal rule: what you're looking for is **the one element that makes this
product and this market unique.** It's inside them and nowhere else. No outside
formula will hand it to you.

And the discipline that follows: no formula works twice, because every
product–market–moment is new. The temptation grows with success — the better
your last headline, the more you'll want to find your next one in your old
files. Argue with success. Including your own.
